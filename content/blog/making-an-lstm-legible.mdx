An LSTM-based response model outperformed our gradient-boosted baseline on sequence data — contact
history, engagement patterns over time — by a comfortable margin. That was never the hard part.
The hard part was getting a model that isn't a decision tree past a review process built around
being able to explain, in a sentence, why a specific customer got a specific decision.

## Attention weights answer "when"

Visualizing the attention weights over the input sequence answers a narrow but useful question:
*which time steps did the model actually look at?* If the model is weighting a channel interaction
from fourteen months ago as heavily as one from last week, that's worth knowing before you ship it
— sometimes it's a real signal, sometimes it's the model latching onto an artifact of how the
sequence was padded.

Attention weights are not, on their own, an explanation of *why* the model made a particular
prediction. They tell you where it looked, not what it concluded from looking there.

## Sequence-level SHAP answers "why"

For the "why," we computed SHAP contributions over the sequence, attributing the final prediction
back to individual time steps and features rather than treating the sequence as one opaque blob.
That's what actually goes in front of a reviewer: not "the model attended to month 11," but "a
spend increase in month 11, combined with a channel switch in month 13, contributed +0.14 to the
response score."

## Using both, deliberately

The two tools answer different questions, and using only one leaves a gap. We paired attention
visualization for debugging — catching sequences the model was attending to in an obviously wrong
way — with SHAP for the artifact that actually accompanies a scoring decision. That combination is
what got the model through governance, and it's the reason the estimated 15% lift in conversion
ever made it into production instead of staying a promising offline result.
