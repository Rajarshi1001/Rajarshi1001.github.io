The offer-ranking system I inherited had three separate XGBoost models, each trained for a
different card segment, each scored independently and then stitched together downstream. It
worked, but every change — a new feature, a retrain, a governance review — had to happen three
times.

The instinct is to just merge the training data and fit one model. That mostly doesn't work: the
segments genuinely respond differently to the same offer, and a single model tends to regress
toward the largest segment's behavior, quietly getting worse for everyone else.

## Response-to-gross as the common currency

Instead of merging models, we changed what the models predicted. Every offer got scored on
**response-to-gross** — expected response scaled by the offer's gross value — instead of a
raw response probability. That gave every segment a comparable currency, so ranking could happen
across segments instead of within them.

## The metric that decided the architecture

The question that actually mattered wasn't "is the new setup as accurate as the old one" — it was
**match rate**: for a given card member, does the new 2-segmentation setup surface the same top
offer the old 3-model setup would have, or a better one by response-to-gross? We held out several
months of live scoring decisions and checked agreement directly, rather than trusting an aggregate
AUC that could hide segment-level regressions.

96% match rate meant the two segmentations reproduced almost every decision the three-model system
made — and where they disagreed, the new ranking's picks scored higher on response-to-gross, not
lower. That's the number that got the migration approved, not a lift in offline accuracy.

## What this generalizes to

Consolidating models is rarely about proving the new one is "better" in the abstract. It's about
proving it makes the *same good decisions* the thing it replaces was already making, on the
specific cases that matter, before anyone will let you touch what happens after.
